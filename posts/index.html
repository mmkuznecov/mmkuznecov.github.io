Several views on cross-entropy

2026-05-31

Cross-entropy is usually handed to you as a formula to memorize. It is friendlier than that. You don't have to invent it — it falls out of one simple question: how probable is the data under my model? The cleanest place to watch it appear is a single coin. Everything after that is the same object seen from a different angle.

1.Start with a coin

Take a biased coin that lands heads with probability $\hat{y}$. You flip it once and see an outcome $y$, where $y = 1$ means heads and $y = 0$ means tails. The probability the model assigned to what actually happened is

$$ P(y) = \hat{y}^{\,y}\,(1 - \hat{y})^{\,1-y}. $$

That looks fancier than it is: plug in $y=1$ and it reads $\hat{y}$; plug in $y=0$ and it reads $1-\hat{y}$. It just picks out whichever probability matches the outcome.

Now flip the coin $n$ times. Because the flips are independent, the probability of the whole sequence is the product of the per-flip probabilities. Products of many numbers below one get tiny and awkward, so we take a logarithm — which turns the product into a sum — and flip the sign, so that smaller means better:

$$ -\log P = -\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\,y_i \log \hat{y}_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{y}_i)\,\Big]. $$
That expression is binary cross-entropy. We never defined a loss by hand — we wrote down the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli (coin) model, and cross-entropy was already there.

This is exactly what logistic regression minimizes, with the coin's bias produced by a sigmoid, $\hat{y} = \sigma(w^\top x)$. So “train with cross-entropy” and “do maximum likelihood on a Bernoulli model” are the same sentence.

2.Why the logarithm earns its place

The log is doing two useful jobs at once. It turns an unwieldy product into a sum, and it punishes confident mistakes hard. If the model puts probability $0.9$ on what really happened, the cost is $-\log 0.9 \approx 0.11$. If it confidently bets $0.01$ on the truth, the cost is $-\log 0.01 \approx 4.61$ — roughly forty times worse. Being wrong and sure about it is what cross-entropy hates most.

3.From a coin to many classes

With more than two outcomes, the target is a one-hot vector $p$ that is $1$ on the true class $y$ and $0$ elsewhere. The general cross-entropy is a sum over classes, but the one-hot target zeroes out every term except one:

$$ H(p, q) = -\sum_k p(k)\,\log q(k) = -\log q(y). $$

Same instruction as the coin, now for a die: put as much probability as you can on the answer that actually occurred.

4.The identity that ties everything together

Step back from samples to whole distributions and a tidy relationship appears:

$$ H(p, q) \;=\; H(p) \;+\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q). $$

Here $H(p)$ is the entropy of the data — the uncertainty you cannot remove — and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ is how far the model $q$ sits from the truth $p$. Since $H(p)$ has nothing to do with your parameters, minimizing cross-entropy is exactly minimizing that distance. The plot makes the split literal: the cross-entropy curve is the flat entropy floor plus the KL gap, and both the loss and the gap bottom out together at $q = p$.

5.The KL derivation, in three lines

Where does that identity come from? Start from the definition of KL divergence and split the logarithm:

$$ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) = \mathbb{E}_p\!\left[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right] = \mathbb{E}_p[\log p(x)] - \mathbb{E}_p[\log q(x)]. $$

The first term is $-H(p)$ and the second is $-H(p,q)$, so $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = H(p,q) - H(p)$ — rearrange and you have the box above.

6.Coding theory

$H(p)$ is the shortest average message length if you knew the true distribution; $H(p,q)$ is the average length when you build your code for $q$ but the world runs on $p$. The gap $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$ is the wasted bits. So cross-entropy = unavoidable uncertainty + a penalty for using the wrong model.

7.Proper scoring rule

Cross-entropy is minimized, in expectation, only when you report the true probabilities — not just the right label. If the true conditional is $p(y\mid x)$, the optimum is $q(y\mid x) = p(y\mid x)$. That is why it is trained against instead of accuracy: accuracy looks only at the top guess, while cross-entropy cares whether your $0.9$ really means $0.9$. (The companion notebook shows temperature scaling lowering cross-entropy while accuracy stays perfectly flat.)

8.Empirical risk and the Bayesian view

In practice you don't know $p$; you have data. Replacing $p$ with the empirical distribution $\hat{p}$ turns cross-entropy into the average negative log-likelihood over your sample — which is maximum likelihood estimation, full stop. Add a prior $p(\theta)$ and the same objective becomes MAP:

$$ \theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\min_\theta \Big[-\sum_i \log p_\theta(x_i) - \log p(\theta)\Big]. $$

The extra $-\log p(\theta)$ is regularization: an L2 penalty is exactly a Gaussian prior on the weights. So weight decay and “a prior belief that weights are small” are the same statement.

9.Soft labels

Targets don't have to be one-hot. When $p$ is a full distribution, cross-entropy $-\sum_k p_k \log q_k$ compares the whole vectors, which is what label smoothing, knowledge distillation, and multi-annotator labels rely on. If a teacher says $p=(0.7,0.2,0.1)$ and the student predicts $q=(0.6,0.3,0.1)$, the loss grades the entire distribution, not just the arg-max — which is why distillation matches soft predictions rather than hard labels.

10.Forward vs. reverse KL

Minimizing cross-entropy minimizes the forward KL, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$, which is heavily charged wherever the data has mass but the model does not. That makes it mode-covering: it spreads the model over every bump in $p$. The reverse direction is mode-seeking and tends to collapse onto one bump — the distinction at the heart of variational inference.

11.Why not just use MSE?

If the coin story felt like a detour, here is the payoff. A tempting shortcut is to treat the label as a number and minimize squared error $(\hat{y}-y)^2$. Why don't we? First, be fair: it is not that MSE is meaningless here. The squared error of a probability is the Brier score, and like cross-entropy it is a proper scoring rule — minimized, in expectation, exactly at the true probability. So both losses point at the right answer.

The problem is the journey, not the destination. Put a sigmoid in front, $\hat{y}=\sigma(z)$, and compare the gradients with respect to the pre-activation $z$ for a positive example:

$$ \text{cross-entropy:}\;\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \sigma(z) - y, \qquad \text{squared error:}\;\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \big(\sigma(z)-y\big)\,\sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big). $$

Squared error carries an extra factor $\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$, and that factor collapses to zero whenever the unit is saturated. So a neuron that is confidently wrong ($y=1$ but $\sigma(z)\approx 0$) receives almost no gradient under MSE — learning stalls precisely when it should be fastest. Cross-entropy has no such factor: its gradient is $\approx 1$ in that regime. The logarithm in cross-entropy exists exactly to cancel the $\sigma'$, because cross-entropy is the Bernoulli likelihood and MSE is not.

There is a second, related point of view: the penalty shape. Cross-entropy shoots to infinity as you grow confident in the wrong answer, while squared error is capped at $1$ — so MSE barely flinches at a disaster that cross-entropy treats as catastrophic.
